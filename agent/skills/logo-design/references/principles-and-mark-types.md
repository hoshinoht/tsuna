# Principles and Mark Types

Adapted from logo-design-skill by Kaan Kızıltuğ (MIT). Read when choosing a mark type, justifying a direction,
or diagnosing why a design "feels off".

## What a logo is

- An **identifier, not an explanation**: a signpost to the organisation and its attitude, not a catalogue of services.
- Logo (the mark) ≠ identity (mark + type, colour, imagery, patterns) ≠ brand (what people perceive).
- Meaning accrues through exposure; a new mark is mostly empty. Pick forms that can hold the intended meaning for decades.

## Principles

1. **Who, what, why first**: client values, audience, real need, and where the business is going in 1–10 years.
2. **Identify, don't explain**: depicting every facet makes a cluttered mark that breaks when the business grows.
3. **Simple, not plain**: reduce until the idea is clear, then make one detail ownable.
4. **Relevant, not literal**: match tone and fit; don't draw the product (a tooth for a dentist is clip art).
5. **Distinct**: know the category's conventions and depart from what blends together, while still reading as the right world.
6. **Memorable**: silhouette and colour are the first memory hooks; one defining feature.
7. **One idea**: explainable in one sentence. A light puzzle (hidden arrow, letter-as-object) rewards attention; a riddle fails.
8. **Small and large**: 16 px favicon and building sign; one colour; reversed; embroidered.
9. **Timeless over trendy**: build on a concept, not an effect or the typeface of the moment.
10. **Foundation of a system**: it will live next to type, photos and patterns; never judge it in a void.
11. **Craft**: exact geometry, optical corrections, consistent weights and spacing.
12. **Strong, not stubborn**: defend the strategy, stay open on details.

Respect existing equity and culturally significant symbols; check unintended political, religious or sexual readings.

## Mark types

| Type | Best when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Wordmark | Short, distinctive, new name; small media budget | Generic stock type; long names shrink badly |
| Lettermark / monogram | Long or hard-to-say names; heritage, fashion, institutions | Woven initials carry little meaning |
| Letterform (one initial) | App icons, tech, finance; compact avatar | Many brands share the letter; needs a twist in the treatment |
| Pictorial | Name/story holds a vivid, ownable image | Literalness, clichés, too much detail |
| Abstract | Idea without an object (speed, connection); diversified firms | Meaningless without heavy exposure |
| Negative space | Two related silhouettes share an edge | Hardest to do; forced results; hidden figure must be a bonus |
| Mascot | Family, food, games, community | Detail, dating, weak at small sizes; make a head-only version |
| Emblem / badge | Institutions, clubs, craft, heritage | Illegible small; plan a simplified inner symbol |
| Combination | Most new brands | Two weak halves ≠ one strong mark; symbol and type must share DNA |

Notes per type:
- **Wordmark**: make it ownable with custom letterforms (cuts, joins, terminals, a distinctive a/g/e), a ligature,
  one letter replaced by a sign, or a shape hidden in counters. Lowercase reads approachable; uppercase reads institutional.
- **Monogram**: letters with rich structure (a, g, k, R, M, W) interlock better than i/l/I; look for shared strokes and nesting counters.
- **Pictorial**: work in silhouette from the most characteristic view; reduce to geometry (a leaf = two arcs). Pair with neutral type.
- **Combination**: symbol and wordmark share stroke weight, radii, angles. Plan horizontal, stacked, symbol-only and
  wordmark-only lockups from day one; wide lockups are the norm for full logos, near-square for icons.

## Decision guide (first strong yes usually wins)

1. Short (≤ ~8 letters), distinctive, unknown name → wordmark or wordmark-led combination.
2. Long, multi-word or hard to pronounce → monogram, or letterform + wordmark.
3. Name or story contains a vivid ownable image → pictorial.
4. Strong idea but no object, and high exposure planned → abstract.
5. Lives mostly as app icon / favicon / avatar → letterform or compact symbol first, then lockup.
6. Heritage, authority, membership, craft → emblem with a simplified inner symbol.
7. Warmth, family, play → mascot or friendly pictorial.

When unsure, explore three different types in round one so the choice is made by comparing real options.

## Colour and type, briefly

- 1–2 colours, ownable in the category, with HEX/RGB/CMYK (and Pantone if vivid); must still work in one colour and greyscale.
- Aim for ≥ 3:1 contrast for logo parts on intended backgrounds; nothing may rely on hue alone.
- Colour cues: blue = trust (and category cliché in finance/tech), green = growth/nature, red = energy, black = premium.
- Max two type families; construct custom letters for ownership; space optically, not metrically.
- Font licences must allow logo use; final masters use outlined paths, never live text.

## Redesigns

- Good reasons: the business changed, the mark fails technically, inconsistent versions circulate, it resembles a
  competitor. Weak reasons: internal boredom, a new executive, chasing a trend.
- **Evolution** (refresh: redraw curves, simplify, retune type, adjust colour, add missing variants) is lower risk than
  **revolution** (new concept). Even revolutions usually keep one anchor, often the colour.
- Start with an **equity audit**: what the public recognises (colour, shape, a letter, a mascot), what must survive,
  what is incidental, what causes problems. Present options along the evolution-revolution spectrum with before/after
  at the same sizes.
